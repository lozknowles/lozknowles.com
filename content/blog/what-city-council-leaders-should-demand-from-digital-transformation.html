For chief executives and council leaders, investment in digital services should answer three questions: will residents get a better service, will staff have less avoidable work, and can the organisation demonstrate that the benefits justify the cost?

Those questions belong at the centre of decisions about budgets, service design and organisational change. They also provide a practical basis for scrutiny: agree the intended outcomes, establish the starting position and publish evidence of progress.

For a resident trying to report a repair, understand a bill or ask for housing help, the test of a council service is straightforward: can I find the right information, complete the task and get a clear response?

That simple expectation sits behind a complicated challenge for smaller city councils. They must maintain everyday services while improving ageing systems, responding to financial pressures and making sure residents can still get help when an online route does not work for them.

Lincoln provides a useful example. Worcester is a particularly close population comparison: both had around 104,000 residents in the ONS's rounded initial 2021 Census results. Exeter, at around 131,000, offers a somewhat larger comparison. These are comparisons between city district councils, whose responsibilities differ from those of county councils and existing unitary authorities. They are useful examples, rather than a performance league table. [1–3]

Lincoln's Vision 2030 connects economic growth, reduced inequality, housing, place and climate with a commitment to accessible and coordinated services. Delivering those ambitions depends on how the organisation works as much as what appears on its website. [4]

The financial context matters. Worcester's 2026/27 budget discussion identified pressures including pay, prices, food-waste collection and property investment, while reporting a balanced medium-term plan. That combination deserves attention: a council can have credible financial arrangements and still face difficult choices about capacity and priorities. [5]

Lincoln's 2024 Productivity Plan acknowledged legacy ICT challenges and described a housing-system replacement spanning more than 180 processes and 15 integrations or interfaces. Those are historical project descriptions, rather than a statement of today's implementation status, but they demonstrate the scale of organisational change involved. [6]

A new form can be useful. Its real value depends on what happens after submission. Does the information reach the right team? Is someone responsible for the case? Can the resident see what happens next? Does an officer have to copy the same information into another system?

The starting point should be a review of the whole journey, including the work residents and staff must do.

Lincoln's accessibility statement acknowledges barriers in parts of the website and some documents. It also describes ongoing monitoring, although the published review date remains June 2023. That date does not establish that testing stopped; it does show why public assurances need to be kept current. [7]

Accessibility needs regular testing with people using keyboards, screen readers, mobile devices and different levels of digital confidence. Telephone and personal support should remain visible and effective. Success includes helping people who need assistance complete the same task with dignity.

Keeping information consistent is another substantial responsibility. In the pages retrieved for this review, Lincoln's reorganisation overview still describes options being explored, while a July 2026 news announcement records the government's decision. [8–9]

This illustrates a practical problem for any council: publishing an announcement should trigger a review of the guidance, forms and frequently asked questions affected by it. Every important page needs an owner, a review date and a clear route for corrections. Communications planning should include what residents need to know before, during and after a service changes.

Complaints offer another source of evidence. Lincoln's 2025/26 report records 648 complaints, compared with 562 the previous year. Responses within target fell from 93% to 86.6%. The report describes increased pressure on processes and capacity, alongside the introduction of a complaints case-management system. [10]

Those are council-wide figures, not evidence that the website caused the increase. Complaint totals also need context about reporting and access. The useful question is whether recurring problems are being identified, remedies completed and service changes shown to work.

The council's published governing-body response recognises the need to strengthen communication, consistency and learning from complaints. That gives residents and scrutiny committees a constructive basis for following progress. [11]

Planned financial benefits deserve the same discipline. Exeter's 2026/27 budget discussion included £500,000 from its digitalisation programme within prior-year savings. A figure in a financial plan needs a clear trail showing when and how the benefit is realised. [12]

For any transformation programme, I would want to see a small set of measures published regularly:

  • Whether residents can complete the most common tasks.
  • How long it takes to resolve a request, including overdue cases.
  • How often people must contact the council again about the same issue.
  • Whether accessibility barriers and outdated information are being removed.
  • Whether promised complaint remedies have actually been completed.
  • Whether savings remain after implementation, support and ongoing operating costs.

The same measures should inform management decisions and scrutiny. Staff need the time, training and authority to improve processes; software alone cannot provide those conditions. Automation can assist with routine administration, but its value should be demonstrated through accurate outcomes and less avoidable work.

Fair assessment also means recognising strengths. Lincoln's external auditor reported no significant weaknesses in its value-for-money arrangements for 2024/25 across financial sustainability, governance, and economy, efficiency and effectiveness. That is meaningful assurance, while leaving room to improve individual services. [13]

Reorganisation adds urgency. The government's July 2026 decision on Lincolnshire set a direction for structural change, subject to Parliamentary approval. Councils must continue serving residents while preparing to bring organisations, information and systems together. [14]

The opportunity for cities of this scale is considerable: clearer information, fewer repeated requests, better coordination and more time for staff to help people with complex needs.

My proposed starting point is a focused 90-day review of a small number of frequently used services. Establish the baseline, identify where requests stall, give each improvement a responsible owner and report the results against the six measures above. The scope should reflect the council's capacity and existing improvement work.

I will be following how these ambitions translate into measurable progress. For residents, the strongest evidence will be a service that is easier to understand, easier to use and dependable when it matters. For leaders, that evidence provides a firmer basis for deciding where to invest next.
